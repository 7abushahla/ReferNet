Every traveler who enters or leaves Dubai presents a travel document that is first checked within seconds, at a counter or a smart gate, and only doubtful documents are referred to experts. \section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The United Arab Emirates is one of the busiest crossroads of international travel. In 2025, Dubai alone recorded more than 65 million passenger crossings through its air, land, and sea ports~\cite{gdrfa2025stats}, each requiring the inspection of a passport, an identity card, or another travel document. At these ports, the General Directorate of Identity and Foreigners Affairs in Dubai (GDRFA Dubai) checks documents in two lines. The first line comprises passport-control counters and smart gates, where documents must be cleared or referred for closer examination within a few seconds~\cite{frontex2023quickcheck}. Referred documents undergo second-line examination at the Document Examination Center (DEC) of GDRFA Dubai, which maintains passport specimens from more than 80 countries~\cite{gulfnews2026forgery}.

A referral, however, delays the traveler's clearance, slows the queue at the counter, and requires specialist examination. At the same time, the scale of detected irregularities underscores the need for careful inspection. Between January 2025 and June 2026, the DEC conducted 20,307 technical examinations of travel documents and found 902 irregularities, including forged documents, manipulated data, and digitally altered images~\cite{gulfnews2026forgery}. These findings show that document fraud remains a serious problem at Dubai's ports, where first-line officers must balance timely clearance with reliable detection.

Furthermore, detecting alterations in travel and identity documents presents particular challenges. A morphed portrait can resemble two individuals, while an altered text field can retain the original font and background, making an altered document appear genuine during a brief inspection. Officers must also assess documents with a wide range of designs from different issuing countries. Document readers assist this process by comparing the presented document with reference information for its design~\cite{regula2023templates}. However, disagreements between officers and readers can complicate the decision to clear or refer a document~\cite{gariup2019firstline}. These challenges make reliable first-line referral decisions particularly difficult.

Recent advances in deep learning (DL) can support document inspection by automatically identifying signs of alteration in document images. Although DL-based detectors have demonstrated strong performance on designs represented during training, their performance often declines substantially on unseen designs~\cite{boned2024sidtd,guan2024idnetarxiv,korshunov2025fantasyid,korshunov2025deepid}. Moreover, single-image detectors assess the presented document without comparing it with a specimen of its design, unlike the reference comparisons used by document examiners and readers~\cite{icao9303p2,regula2023templates}. Reference-based document detectors have also been proposed~\cite{ghanmi2021checksim,chen2022domain}, but these methods have not been evaluated for alteration detection on public datasets with test designs excluded from training.

In this study, we investigate whether conditioning an alteration detector on a reference of the document's design improves first-line referral decisions on designs unseen during training. Given the data page of a presented document, the goal is to determine whether it should be cleared or referred for closer examination. To accomplish this, we adapt a DL-based change-detection network that compares the presented data page with a reference image of the same document type and version. A mask identifies the zones where personal data are expected to differ. We compare the same detector with and without reference information to measure the effect of reference conditioning. In addition to detection performance, we evaluate the resulting referral decisions on unseen designs and measure the processing time per document on a desktop-class central processing unit (CPU).

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:problem} states the research problem, our objectives, and our contributions. Section~\ref{sec:framework} presents the conceptual framework and reviews the literature. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the data, the architecture, the evaluation protocols, and the time measurement. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports and discusses the results, while Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} concludes the paper with recommendations for GDRFA Dubai.

\section{Research Problem, Significance and Objectives}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Research problem}
The first line must refer or clear a travel document within seconds, for designs from more than 80 countries, using readers whose error rates on unseen designs we could not find in any publication. Learned detectors do not solve this problem either. Four benchmarks measure how they degrade on designs absent from training~\cite{boned2024sidtd,guan2024idnetarxiv,korshunov2025fantasyid,korshunov2025deepid}, yet none of the detectors evaluated there can compare a document with the specimen of its design. To the best of our knowledge, no study has given a learned alteration detector such a specimen and measured the effect on those benchmarks against the same detector without it. This study provides that measurement and reports it in the terms of the first line, namely how many genuine documents are referred when a fixed share of altered ones may be missed, and how long a decision takes.

\subsection{Significance}
For GDRFA Dubai, the study offers three things. First, it measures the error of a detector on designs that it has never seen, for a detector that handles a new design as the counter already does, by adding the design's specimen to its library rather than by collecting new training data and retraining the model. Second, it releases the protocol and code needed to repeat this measurement on the DEC's own template library, without any document leaving the DEC. Third, it expresses the result as a referral decision and its time cost, the terms in which an operator can act on it. For the research field, it offers, to the best of our knowledge, the first measurement of specimen conditioning for alteration detection on designs held out from training on public data, with the specimen as the only difference between the method and its baseline. Reference-based document detectors themselves have been proposed before, for example CheckSim~\cite{ghanmi2021checksim} and the detector of Chen et al.~\cite{chen2022domain}.

\subsection{Objectives and research questions}
The aim of this study is to establish, on public data, whether giving a learned alteration detector a reference of the presented document's design lets it generalize to designs that it has never seen, and to report the result as a first-line referral decision with its time cost. We address five research questions.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*.}]
  \item How much does detection performance degrade when a detector without a reference meets designs absent from training, and how much of that degradation does the reference recover when it is the only difference?
  \item How large is the gap between an unseen design from a familiar source (Protocol B) and an unseen source with a new generator, capture, and script (Protocol C)?
  \item Which reference (a registered design median or a blank specimen) and which fusion (difference or attention) improve detection performance?
  \item When a referral threshold chosen on the training designs is applied unchanged to unseen designs, which miss and referral rates result?
  \item Can the detector decide within a first-line time budget on a desktop-class CPU, and how much time does the reference add?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Contributions}
Our contributions are summarized as follows.
\begin{itemize}
  \item We provide, to the best of our knowledge, the first measurement of specimen conditioning for alteration detection on designs held out from training on public data, under three protocols and against the same network without the specimen.
  \item We adapt ChangerEx~\cite{fang2023changer}, a change-detection network, to identity documents through registration to the design, a design-median reference, and a channel that marks the personal-data zones, and we add a referral head.
  \item We turn the detector's score into a refer-or-clear decision with a threshold chosen on the training designs, and report the miss and referral rates that it yields on unseen designs.
  \item We measure the time per document and the throughput of every variant on a desktop-class CPU.
  \item We release the references, masks, splits, protocol files, and code, so that GDRFA Dubai can repeat the measurement on its own template library.
\end{itemize}

\section{Theoretical and Conceptual Framework and Literature Review}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Conceptual framework}
Our framework rests on three ideas, namely how travel documents are inspected at a border, how a detector's score becomes a decision, and why a detector should compare a document with a reference.

\textbf{Inspection at the border.} Border inspection distinguishes the first-line check, which every traveler passes, from second-line checks, which are further checks carried out in a separate place when the first line has a doubt~\cite{eu2022handbook}. ICAO Doc~9303\footnote{ICAO Doc 9303, Machine Readable Travel Documents, is the international specification for passports and other travel documents (\url{https://www.icao.int/publications/doc-series/doc-9303}).} likewise defines three levels of document inspection, namely a cursory examination for rapid inspection at the point of usage, an examination by trained inspectors with simple equipment, and an inspection by forensic specialists~\cite{icao9303p2}. Dubai's three tiers, with first-line officers, a second line, and the DEC, follow these levels~\cite{thenational2024fake}. To verify a document, inspectors compare it with a specimen, a sample of the same document type and version that the issuing State provides to other States for reference~\cite{icao9303p2}. The first line therefore makes a binary decision, either to clear the document or to refer it to the second line, within seconds and with the document reader as its only instrument.

\textbf{From a score to a decision.} A detector serves this decision through a threshold. Let $s$ be the detector's score that a presented document is altered, $t$ a threshold, and $p$ the prevalence, that is, the share of altered documents among those presented. The first line refers a document when $s > t$. Two error rates then describe the decision. The miss rate $M(t) = P(s \le t \mid \text{altered})$ is the share of altered documents that are cleared, and $P(s > t \mid \text{genuine})$ is the share of genuine documents that are referred. In the vocabulary of ISO/IEC~30107-3~\cite{iso30107}, which the document-forgery benchmarks use~\cite{korshunov2025fantasyid}, these are the attack presentation classification error rate (APCER) and the bona fide presentation classification error rate (BPCER). Since genuine documents make up a share $1-p$ of the traffic and altered ones a share $p$, the law of total probability gives the share of all documents that the first line refers,
\begin{equation}
R(t) = (1-p)\,P(s>t \mid \text{genuine}) + p\,P(s>t \mid \text{altered}).
\label{eq:referral}
\end{equation}
The two error rates are standard, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:referral} only combines them into the quantity that sets the workload of the second line. Raising $t$ lowers $R$ but raises $M$, so an operator fixes the largest acceptable miss rate, the miss budget, and accepts the referral rate that comes with it (see Fig.~\ref{fig:referral}). The prevalence $p$ is small and unknown, so $R(t)$ is driven almost entirely by the genuine documents that are referred. We therefore choose $t$ on the training designs and keep it fixed on designs never seen, and we compute the workload of the second line only for assumed values of $p$.

\textbf{Comparison against a reference.} Comparing a document with a specimen turns forgery detection into change detection, the task of finding where two registered images of the same object differ. Change detection differs from anomaly detection, which flags samples that look unlike normal data without any paired reference, and from out-of-distribution detection, which flags inputs that come from a different distribution than the training data. The same principle of comparison appears in differential morphing-attack detection, which compares a passport photograph with a trusted live capture of the traveler~\cite{scherhag2020dmad,borghi2021double}, and in the inspection of printed circuit boards, where each board is compared with an image of a defect-free board, the golden sample~\cite{fridman2021changechip}. A detector that sees a single image must learn both what each design looks like and how an alteration departs from it, and only the first of these is specific to a design. A registered specimen supplies the design at inference time, so the network only has to learn how an altered field departs from its design, which is the same on every design. Unlike a circuit board, however, a document's personal-data fields are supposed to differ from the specimen, so we pair the reference with a zone mask that marks where differences are expected (see Fig.~\ref{fig:idea}).

\subsection{Literature review}
We review four strands of work, namely detectors evaluated on public benchmarks, detectors that compare a document with a reference, systems designed for deployment, and the change-detection methods from which our architecture comes. For each strand, we note what it lacks for the first line.

\subsubsection{Single-image detectors and their measured collapse on unseen designs}
Four benchmarks evaluate alteration detection on designs absent from training, and every detector evaluated on them degrades there. SIDTD~\cite{boned2024sidtd} forges the ten designs of MIDV-2020~\cite{bulatov2022midv2020}. Most of its baseline classifiers exceed 96\% accuracy on templates of the designs they were trained on, whereas its few-shot models reach 82.0\% to 91.0\% on four held-out nationalities, even though they are evaluated on video frames and each test episode supplies five labeled genuine and five labeled forged examples of the held-out nationality. In IDNet~\cite{guan2024idnetarxiv}, a detector trained on the West Virginia driver's license and tested on the Arizona one falls from 98\% to 100\% accuracy to 50.6\% on face morphing and 60.9\% on text replacement. On the printed and photographed cards of FantasyID~\cite{korshunov2025fantasyid}, TruFor~\cite{guillaro2023trufor} reaches AUCs of 99.5\% and 99.9\% on two text attacks but 55.8\% on face swaps, and at a threshold set on validation data, it misses 4.9\% of the attacks while rejecting 62.0\% of the genuine cards. In the DeepID challenge~\cite{korshunov2025deepid}, the winning F1-score fell from 0.991 on fantasy cards to 0.719 on 20,000 private real documents, and a 2026 survey by Das et al.~\cite{das2026survey} found an APCER above 24\% on unseen templates for every public model that it tested, including GPT-5 and Gemini. Similarly, DocVerify~\cite{diazrodriguez2026docverify}, trained on FantasyID alone, falls to an AUC of 0.50 on the SIDTD templates. All of these detectors judge a single image, except CoAARC in SIDTD~\cite{boned2024sidtd}, which pairs the query with a randomly drawn genuine document rather than a specimen of its own design and performs worst, at 62.8\%. None of them can compare a document with the specimen of its design, and none reports its time per document.

\subsubsection{Reference-conditioned detectors for documents}
Commercial document readers check each document against the specimen of its design with checks configured for that design, and we found no published error rate for these checks~\cite{regula2023templates}. Learned detectors that take a reference as a second input are rarer, and each addresses a different question. CheckSim~\cite{ghanmi2021checksim} measures the similarity between an aligned document and a reference image of its document model, on four types of French identity documents from a private dataset. Chen et al.~\cite{chen2022domain} compare a questioned document with genuine references of the same template to detect recapture, that is, a genuine document printed or displayed again and photographed, rather than altered content, and they reach an APCER of 6.92\% at a BPCER of 5\% across document types. Al-Ghadi et al.~\cite{alghadi2022guilloche} compare the guilloche pattern of a document, the fine interlaced lines of its background, with that of a genuine document of the same country, and in their follow-up~\cite{alghadi2023fmidv}, which classifies a single image at test time without the reference, the AUC falls to between 50\% and 56\%. Neitthoffer et al.~\cite{neitthoffer2023knowledge} gave a text-recognition network the template of each document type and found that fusing the template through attention generalized to unseen types, with a character error rate of 0.31, whereas adding it as an extra input channel did not, with a character error rate of 0.81. Most recently, Nieto-Hidalgo et al.~\cite{nietohidalgo2026zeroshot} reduced the equal error rate of presentation-attack detection on unseen countries from 26.56\% to 9.07\% by giving the model four genuine samples of the target card. None of these detectors is evaluated on held-out designs for alterations with public data, none outputs a map of the altered region, and none reports its time per document.

\subsubsection{Deployment-oriented systems}
EdgeDoc~\cite{george2025edgedoc} combines a lightweight convolutional backbone with transformer blocks to detect and localize alterations, FLiD~\cite{flid2026} classifies individual fields with a frozen MobileNetV3-Small backbone and a head of 191K parameters, and Idakto's embedded framework~\cite{idakto2025embedded} matches documents against a reference library of 128 countries, which its authors describe as allowing ``near real-time operation''. These systems are designed to be lightweight, yet we found no measured inference time, throughput, or CPU latency for any of them.

\subsubsection{Where the architecture comes from}
In remote sensing, change detection compares two co-registered satellite images with Siamese networks, which process both images with shared weights and subtract their features~\cite{daudt2018fcsiamdiff}. Changer~\cite{fang2023changer} added interaction between the two feature streams and an alignment step based on a learned flow, and the Open-CD toolbox~\cite{li2025opencd} provides these networks with pretrained weights. In industry, optical inspection compares each printed circuit board with its golden sample~\cite{fridman2021changechip}, and in biometrics, differential morphing-attack detection compares a probe image with a trusted reference~\cite{scherhag2020dmad,borghi2021double}. None of this work concerns identity documents. Moreover, these methods treat every difference from the reference as a defect, whereas the personal-data fields of a document are supposed to differ from its specimen.

\section{Methodology and Procedures}
\label{sec:method}
This section describes the study population and samples, the data preparation, the proposed architecture, the evaluation protocols and metrics, and the time measurement, which together form the population, samples, and tools that the award's guide asks for. Three public datasets, SIDTD~\cite{boned2024sidtd}, FantasyID~\cite{korshunov2025fantasyid}, and IDNet~\cite{guan2024idnet}, are registered to their designs and paired with a reference of the same design and a zone mask. ChangerEx~\cite{fang2023changer} is extended into a reference-conditioned detector, and every variant is trained alongside the same network without a reference. All variants are evaluated under four protocols, the first of which repeats the published protocol of SIDTD to anchor our results to prior work, and the speed of the detector is estimated on a desktop-class CPU.

\subsection{Study population and samples}
\label{sec:population}
The population of interest is the identity and travel documents presented at a first-line check. Real travelers' documents can neither be used nor released, so we sample this population through three public datasets that pair genuine documents with digitally altered versions of them (see Table~\ref{tab:data} and Fig.~\ref{fig:attacks}). SIDTD also provides the published protocol on which we anchor our results. Beyond this anchor, the three datasets test three ways in which a counter meets a design that it has not seen, namely a new design from a familiar source, a new source with a new generator, capture, and script, and a published pair of designs.

\subsection{Tools: data preparation}
\label{sec:prep}
Every image becomes a record of its design, identity, label, attack type, capture device, field rectangles, mask of the altered region, and group of related images. We use the forgeries as the datasets provide them and create none of our own.

\textbf{Registration.} Registration places every document in the same position as the reference of its design, so that a pixel-wise comparison is meaningful. Each image is first scaled onto a 512~$\times$~800 canvas, with padding that keeps its proportions. It is then aligned to its design in two passes and without any annotation. Each pass matches SIFT keypoints~\cite{lowe2004sift} between the image and the design and estimates a similarity transform, that is, a rotation, a scale, and a shift, with RANSAC~\cite{fischler1981ransac}, which discards false matches. The masks of the altered regions and the zone masks are moved with the same transforms. An image is aligned only if at least 40 keypoint matches agree with the transform, and every image in our data met this requirement, with at least 214 matches. FantasyID's photographs needed corrections of up to 2.8 degrees and 24 pixels, whereas SIDTD's templates moved by less than one pixel.

\textbf{References.} The default reference of a design is the pixel-wise median over its registered genuine documents (see Fig.~\ref{fig:references}). The median keeps what all documents of the design share, such as the background pattern, headings, field labels, and borders, and it averages out the personal data that differ from one holder to the next. To keep a query's own document out of its reference, the median never includes the query's identity. In Protocol A, it is computed for each fold from the genuine templates of the design in that fold's training file. In Protocols B and C, it is computed from the genuine documents of the design other than those of the query's identity, and in Protocol D from the genuine images of the design outside the test set. The network of Protocol B therefore never trains on the design of a test document and receives its median only as an input at test time. Real specimens are not public, and none of the forgery datasets includes one, so the median serves as our public stand-in for a specimen. For the ten SIDTD designs and the two IDNet designs, we also test a second kind of reference, the blank templates that the IDNet authors produced by digitally removing the personal data from one public sample of each design~\cite{guan2024idnetarxiv}, registered to our medians, which serve as blank specimens. FantasyID has no such template.

\textbf{Zone and ground-truth masks.} The zone mask of a design marks its personal-data fields, namely the portrait, the text fields, and the signature, as the union of the field rectangles of its genuine documents, enlarged by 4 pixels. The ground-truth mask of a forgery is the rectangle of its altered region, taken from SIDTD's field rectangle enlarged by 8 pixels, from IDNet's bounding boxes, and from FantasyID's rectangles marked as altered.

\textbf{Training pairs.} Each training example pairs a document with the reference of its design. Genuine documents are labeled to be cleared and altered ones to be referred. In addition, 10\% of the genuine training documents are paired with the reference of a different design and labeled to be referred, with the whole document as the altered region. These examples teach the network that a reference that does not match the document is itself a reason to refer, so that a document matched to the wrong design is not cleared by mistake. The control without a reference receives no such pairs, since it cannot see the reference and they would only teach it to refer genuine documents.

\textbf{Compression control.} The JPEG quality of a file turns out to reveal its label in both SIDTD and FantasyID. SIDTD stores every genuine template at quality 92 and every forgery at quality 75. The forgeries take this quality from the generator released with the dataset~\cite{boned2024sidtd}, which saves them without setting a quality and therefore at its image library's default quality of 75. SIDTD's cropped video clips, which we do not use, carry a similar cue. In every clip file that we sampled, the genuine frames are JPEG images and the forged ones are PNG images with a .jpg extension. On these clips, the recurrent few-shot detector of Talarmain et al.~\cite{talarmain2024fewshot} reaches AUCs of 99.9\% to 100\% on held-out nationalities. FantasyID stores its genuine cards at quality 93.5 to 95 and most attacks at quality 75. The quality alone therefore separates genuine from altered images in every split of Protocols A, B, and C (see Table~\ref{tab:jpeg}), so a detector could score perfectly without looking at the document. The DeepID challenge reported this pattern for FantasyID's training data~\cite{korshunov2025deepid}. For SIDTD, however, we did not find it reported, neither in the dataset paper~\cite{boned2024sidtd} nor in the later papers of Talarmain et al.~\cite{talarmain2024fewshot} and Díaz Rodríguez~\cite{diazrodriguez2026docverify} or the preprint of Webster and Scheirer~\cite{webster2026saliency}, which use its clips or templates. Following the SIDTD paper, we read the raw files as distributed, store our canvases without loss as PNG images, and use no JPEG augmentation, so that our networks are trained and tested on the same files as the published models. Our protocol therefore reports every result on the raw files. To measure how much a published detector relies on the compression, we also score the released models of the SIDTD authors after re-encoding every test file at quality 75 before their own preprocessing. Genuine and altered files then share one compression level, so a detector that relies on the compression falls toward chance. \todo{Following commit e6b986d (11 Oct), our own networks are reported on the raw files only. The 30 Protocol A runs also have results with the query and reference canvases re-encoded at quality 75 (AUC 0.571 for the control, 0.928 for the median, and 0.896 for the blank specimen), which can be restored if wanted.}

\subsection{Tools: proposed architecture}
\label{sec:arch}
ChangerEx~\cite{fang2023changer} is a Siamese change-detection network for satellite images, available with pretrained weights in the Open-CD toolbox~\cite{li2025opencd}. We build on it for two reasons. Change detection has the same structure as our task, two registered images and the question of where they differ, and ChangerEx aligns the two feature maps before comparing them, which absorbs the small misalignment that remains after registration. To our knowledge, ChangerEx has not been applied to documents before, and the building blocks that we keep come from published work~\cite{daudt2018fcsiamdiff,fang2023changer}. We use the network as it is and test an attention variant as an ablation (see Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}).

\textbf{Standard network.} The query $x$ and the reference $r$, each with the zone mask $z$ appended as a fourth channel, pass through a shared ResNet-18 encoder~\cite{he2016resnet}. After the second, third, and fourth stages, ChangerEx exchanges alternate columns or channels between the two streams, which lets each stream see the other without extra parameters. The decoder then combines the four scales of each image, predicts a small displacement field for each image, warps each feature map by it, and subtracts the other image's features, a step that its authors call flow dual-alignment fusion. The difference is near zero where the document matches its design and non-zero at an altered field, on any design, and the zone channel tells the network where personal data are allowed to differ. A classifier turns the result into an alteration map $M$ at a quarter of the input resolution. A referral head that we add pools the fused features over the whole image, appends the largest value of $M$, and maps the result through two dense layers to the score $s$.

\textbf{Attention variant.} The attention variant adds, at the two deepest stages, a self-attention block over the query's features and a cross-attention block in which the query's features attend to the reference's features~\cite{vaswani2017attention}, so that each location of the document can look up what its design shows there. A final attention step relates the pooled global feature to the local features before the referral score. Each block has six heads with a key dimension of 32, and its output projection starts at zero, so that the variant starts training as the standard network. This mirrors the attention path of our writer-identification architecture~\cite{abushahla2026ijdar} and follows Neitthoffer et al.~\cite{neitthoffer2023knowledge}, who found attention necessary where channel stacking failed.

\textbf{Control without a reference.} To isolate the effect of the reference, the standard network is also trained as a control. The control receives a content-free reference canvas, a uniform image at the mean color of the ImageNet normalization, together with the query's zone mask, so it has the same architecture and parameters as the method but never sees the design. It is trained without wrong-design pairs. In the control runs reported here, however, the feature exchange was still active, so after the second, third, and fourth stages, half of the query's features were replaced by those of the content-free canvas. We have therefore implemented a fairer control that switches the exchange off, so that the query keeps all of its features with the same parameters, and it will replace the current control in the final results. \todo{Fair control with the exchange off (10 runs of Protocol A) pending.}

\textbf{Training.} We start from the ChangerEx weights trained on the LEVIR-CD change-detection dataset~\cite{chen2020levir} and widen the first convolution to four input channels with zero weights for the zone channel, so that the network initially behaves as the original one. These weights were trained from random initialization rather than from ImageNet, and their authors selected them on the LEVIR-CD test split, so we use them only as a starting point. The loss has two parts. The first is the binary cross-entropy between the referral score and the label of the document. The second supervises the alteration map pixel by pixel, as the sum of a binary cross-entropy and a Dice loss over the valid region of the canvas. The Dice loss~\cite{milletari2016vnet} is one minus a soft version of the Dice coefficient~\cite{dice1945},
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Dice}} = 1 - \frac{2\sum_i p_i\, g_i + 1}{\sum_i p_i + \sum_i g_i + 1},
\label{eq:dice}
\end{equation}
where $p_i$ is the predicted probability that pixel $i$ is altered, $g_i$ is 1 inside the true altered region and 0 elsewhere, and the sums run over the valid pixels of all the documents in a batch. The coefficient measures the overlap between the predicted and the true altered regions, from 0 when they do not overlap to 1 when they coincide. An altered field covers only a small part of a document, so a pixel-wise cross-entropy alone is dominated by the many unaltered pixels and can be lowered by predicting no alteration anywhere. The Dice loss depends only on the overlap with the altered region, so it rewards finding that region whatever its size, and EdgeDoc~\cite{george2025edgedoc} combines the two losses in the same way to localize forgeries in identity documents. The 1 added to the numerator and the denominator keeps the loss defined for batches that contain no altered pixel. We weight the loss of the map three times as much as the loss of the score, which gives the dense supervision of the map more influence on training than the single label of each document. Table~\ref{tab:train} lists the other settings. Training ran on NVIDIA A10G GPUs of the high-performance computing cluster of the American University of Sharjah, and one run took about an hour on one GPU. We train every model for the full schedule without early stopping. As the SIDTD paper does, we keep the checkpoint with the best accuracy on the validation split, and the validation AUC breaks ties.

\subsection{Evaluation protocols and metrics}
\label{sec:protocols}
We evaluate under four protocols (see Table~\ref{tab:protocols}). Protocol A repeats the published protocol of SIDTD, in which every design appears in training, and anchors our network to prior work. Protocols B, C, and D then hold the test designs out of training. As in our writer-identification study~\cite{abushahla2026ijdar}, with its line-level and page-disjoint protocols, the difference between protocols is itself a result. In every protocol, the reference of a document never contains the document itself.

\textbf{Protocol A, the anchor on SIDTD's published protocol.} Protocol A follows the template protocol of the SIDTD paper~\cite{boned2024sidtd} exactly. Its ten official folds divide the 1,000 genuine and 1,222 forged templates into training, validation, and test files of about 1,778, 222, and 222 images, and we use them as given, so every design appears in training. As in Table~5 of Boned et al.~\cite{boned2024sidtd}, we report the accuracy at a threshold of 0.5 and the AUC, as the mean and standard deviation over the ten folds. Only the method differs from theirs, in three respects. First, our network sees a 512~$\times$~800 canvas that keeps the proportions of the document, whereas the published models see an image resized to 299~$\times$~299 pixels, or to 224~$\times$~224 for ViT-L/16 and CoAARC. An altered field therefore has a median height of 33 pixels in our input, against 20 pixels at 299~$\times$~299. Second, we use small shifts and photometric jitter but no flips, which would break the alignment with the reference, whereas the published models also use random resized crops and flips. Third, we train with the settings of Table~\ref{tab:train} rather than for 100 epochs at a learning rate of 0.01 with a batch size of 32. Before any gain from the reference is claimed, our control has to reach the published single-image results, namely an AUC of at least 0.99 and an accuracy of at least 97\%, the range of the published ResNet50 and EfficientNet-B3. This gate validates our data, folds, scoring, and network against prior work.

\textbf{Protocol B, an unseen design from a familiar source.} Protocol B keeps everything from Protocol A and changes only the split. It uses the ten groups of four nationalities that the SIDTD authors hold out in their few-shot experiments~\cite{boned2024sidtd}. In each group, the network trains on the templates of the other six nationalities, with about 10\% of their identities held out for validation, and it is tested on the four nationalities that it has never seen. We report the mean and standard deviation over the ten groups, together with the results for each test nationality. SIDTD's published few-shot results on these groups were measured on video frames and with five labeled genuine and five labeled forged examples of each test nationality, so they are not comparable to ours, and our own control on the same split is the comparison.

\textbf{Protocol C, an unseen source, generator, capture, and script.} We train on all ten SIDTD designs and test on FantasyID~\cite{korshunov2025fantasyid} without any training on it, so all 13 of its designs are unseen. The main result is on its three held-out designs, with their 300 genuine cards and 149 text rewrites (Attack-3). Attack-1, the 786 text rewrites of its ten other designs, forms a second result, with the Arabic cards reported separately. FantasyID's test set has no genuine cards of these ten designs, so Attack-1 is compared both with the 300 held-out genuine cards, as in the published results, and with the genuine cards of the same designs from FantasyID's training folder, which our model never sees. \todo{Protocols C and D are revisited in their own plan after Protocols A and B. Confirm the Protocol C training set (SIDTD only, or with IDNet AZ and WV) now that face attacks are out of scope.}

\textbf{Protocol D, a published pair of designs.} We train on IDNet's West Virginia design and test on its Arizona design, and the reverse, as in Table~14 of Guan et al.~\cite{guan2024idnetarxiv}. Following their split of 4,979 training and 1,000 test identities, we take the last 1,000 identities in sorted file order as the test set, since the paper does not state the order. We report accuracy at their threshold of 0.5. \todo{Confirm which IDNet tasks Protocol D reports now that face attacks are out of scope, and whether IDNet's text replacement copies fields between documents of the same design, which a reference cannot reveal, as with SIDTD's crop-and-replace.}

\textbf{Metrics.} The area under the ROC curve (AUC) measures how well the scores rank altered documents above genuine ones over all possible thresholds. It compares detectors without fixing an operating point, and most published results report it. However, it says nothing about the threshold that an officer would use, and it does not reveal whether the scores of different designs share a common scale~\cite{fawcett2006roc}. We therefore also report error rates at operating points, namely the equal error rate (EER), at which the miss rate equals the share of genuine documents referred, APCER at a BPCER of 1\% and 5\%, the half total error rate (HTER) at FantasyID's published operating point, and accuracy at a threshold of 0.5 where a published result uses it~\cite{iso30107}. At the pixel level, the F1-score of the alteration map measures whether the detector finds the altered field. It is computed for each document, over the altered pixels of an altered document and over the unaltered pixels of a genuine one, and averaged over the test documents, so that a map that marks nothing on a genuine document scores 1. For the decision itself, the main measure is the transported threshold. We choose the largest threshold whose miss rate on the validation split of the training designs is at most 5\%, keep it fixed, and report the miss and referral rates that it yields on the test set, which in Protocols B, C, and D contains only unseen designs. We report the workload of the second line only for assumed prevalences of 0.1\%, 1\%, and 5\%.

\textbf{Statistics and ablations.} Each result of Protocols A and B is the mean and standard deviation over the ten folds or groups, with one run each at seed 42. For the difference between the method and its control, we also compute a 95\% confidence interval with a paired bootstrap~\cite{efron1993bootstrap}. We repeatedly resample the test set with replacement, keeping each group of related documents together, score both models on the same resample, and take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resulting differences in AUC. Ablations compare the registered median with the blank specimen and the standard network with the attention variant. Further ablations, with one seed each, use a single genuine card as the reference, an unregistered median, a network without the zone channel, and the input size of the published models. The runs proceed in priority order, Protocol A first, then Protocols B, C, and D.

Our control fails the gate (see Table~\ref{tab:resA}). Without a reference, the standard network reaches an accuracy of 57.2\% and an AUC of 0.60, far below the 98.1\% to 99.4\% and the AUCs of 0.99 to 1.00 of the published ResNet50 and EfficientNet-B3. Given the registered design median, the same network reaches 92.3\% and an AUC of 0.94, and given the blank specimen, 90.3\% and 0.92. Against the current control, the reference therefore raises the AUC of our network by more than 0.3 even on designs seen in training, but no arm of our network reaches the published single-image results.

To locate this gap, we scored the released checkpoints of the two strongest published models on the same folds, with the authors' own preprocessing. Both reproduce the published results, with accuracies of 98.1\% and 99.4\% and an AUC of 1.000, so our copy of the data, the folds, and the scoring agrees with that of the SIDTD authors. When every test file is re-encoded at quality 75, however, the same checkpoints fall to accuracies of 57.2\% and 55.2\% and AUCs of 0.655 and 0.539, close to our control. The published models therefore separate genuine from forged templates almost entirely by the compression of the files, which reveals the label in every fold (see Table~\ref{tab:jpeg}). Our network, by contrast, responds to the alteration rather than to the compression, as the results by attack type below show.

The gate assumed that the published results measure the detection of alterations, and the diagnostic shows that they mostly measure the compression of the files. An independent single-image detector also stays far below the published rows on these templates. Retrained on an 80/20 split of them, at 224~$\times$~224 pixels and without augmentation, DocVerify~\cite{diazrodriguez2026docverify} reaches an AUC of 0.83. The gate therefore cannot validate our control as planned, and two further controls will complete the comparison. The fair control, without the feature exchange (see Section~\ref{sec:arch}), measures how well our network detects alterations from a single image. A second control is trained on canvases that are only letterboxed and not registered, so that each image is resampled once, as the published models resize it once, and it measures how much of the published accuracy our network reaches when it can use the compression cue as the published models do. \todo{Fair control and with-cue control (10 runs each): pending. Paired bootstrap interval of the AUC difference between the median and the fair control.}

By attack type, the median reference detects inpaint-and-rewrite forgeries almost perfectly, with an AUC of 0.995, against 0.61 for the control. It cannot detect crop-and-replace forgeries, however, with an AUC of 0.48, and neither can the blank specimen or the control, with 0.55 and 0.52. A crop-and-replace forgery pastes a field from another document of the same design, so the pasted field matches the design in font, color, and layout, and a comparison with the design cannot reveal it. Both kinds of forgery are stored at quality 75, so a detector that relied on the compression would flag them alike. The contrast between them therefore also shows that our network responds to the alteration rather than to the compression of the file. The alteration map also finds the altered field. Its F1-score is 0.90 with the median and 0.88 with the blank specimen, against 0.51 for the control.

The gain holds on every design. With the median, the AUC ranges from 0.885 on the Azerbaijani passport to 0.960 on the Greek passport, whereas the control ranges from 0.50 to 0.71. The blank specimen comes within 0.05 of the median on every design and within 0.02 overall, even though the IDNet authors produced it from a single public sample of each design rather than from documents of the same kind as the test images. This matters for deployment, since a specimen library holds one specimen of each design rather than many genuine documents.

\subsection{Referral analysis}
In Protocol A, the threshold chosen on each fold's validation file for a 5\% miss rate keeps its miss rate on the test file, but it refers many genuine documents. With the median, it misses 5.5\% of the altered test documents and refers 54\% of the genuine ones, against 4.8\% and 90\% for the control. This share varies strongly between folds, from 10\% to 80\%, and between designs, from 3\% of the genuine Albanian identity cards to 97\% of the Azerbaijani passports. At the equal error rate, which is set on the test documents themselves, the median misses and refers about 11\% each. The scores therefore rank documents well, but their level depends on the design even when every design is seen in training, so the operating point has to be set for each design (RQ4). \subsection{Discussion and limitations}
\todo{Discussion of the results across protocols once Protocols B, C, and D are in.}

\textbf{Limitations.} Several limitations bound what these measurements show. In Protocol A, every design is seen in training and the compression of the files reveals their label, so its results anchor our network to prior work rather than measure how it generalizes. All training forgeries come from two generators, and capture is tested only by FantasyID, so the results describe robustness to a change of design, source, and capture rather than to a skilled forger. SIDTD's genuine images are edited mock documents, IDNet is used in its reduced public release, our blank templates were produced digitally by the IDNet authors rather than issued by States as specimens, and the masks are boxes. A comparison with the design cannot reveal a field copied from another document of the same design, as the crop-and-replace forgeries show. Face swaps and morphs are outside the scope of this paper, and their natural reference is the traveler's live face rather than the specimen~\cite{scherhag2020dmad}. Finally, the DEC's ratio of irregularities is not a prevalence, so workload can only be projected for assumed prevalences, and a detector that sees an RGB image cannot verify ultraviolet, infrared, or chip features, which remain the task of the reader.
