Every traveler who enters or leaves Dubai presents a travel document that is first checked within seconds, at a counter or a smart gate, and only doubtful documents are referred to experts. Between January 2025 and June 2026, the Document Examination Center of GDRFA Dubai found 902 irregularities in 20,307 examined documents, including digitally altered images. Deep-learning detectors find such alterations almost perfectly on the document designs they were trained on but fail on new designs, and since they look at a single image, they cannot check a document against the specimen of its design that border readers already store. \section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The United Arab Emirates is one of the busiest crossroads of international travel. In 2025, more than 65 million travelers crossed the air, land, and sea ports of Dubai alone~\cite{gdrfa2025stats}, and each of them presented a travel document. At these ports, the General Directorate of Identity and Foreigners Affairs in Dubai (GDRFA Dubai) checks documents in two lines. The first line consists of the passport officers at the counters and the smart gates that every traveler passes, and it decides within seconds whether to clear a document or to refer it for closer examination. The second line examines the referred documents, ultimately at the Document Examination Center (DEC) of GDRFA Dubai, a forensic laboratory accredited under ISO/IEC~17025\footnote{ISO/IEC 17025 sets the general requirements for the competence, impartiality, and consistent operation of testing and calibration laboratories (\url{https://www.iso.org/ISO-IEC-17025-testing-and-calibration-laboratories.html}).} that keeps specimens of passports from more than 80 countries~\cite{gulfnews2026forgery}.

In 2025, the first line was reported to number more than 1,600 officers, against about 50 examiners at the DEC~\cite{uaejournal2025fake}. Most documents are therefore judged where time is shortest. Front-line officers ``have only seconds'' for each document~\cite{frontex2023quickcheck}, whereas the DEC can examine a referred document in depth. Between January 2025 and June 2026, the DEC conducted 20,307 technical examinations of travel documents and found 902 irregularities, ``including forged documents, manipulated data, and digitally altered images''~\cite{gulfnews2026forgery}. These irregularities are about 4.4\% of the examined documents. This share is not the share of altered documents among all travelers, and it cannot include the altered documents that the first line cleared, since those were never examined. Digital alterations are hard to see at the speed of the first line (see Fig.~\ref{fig:attacks}). A morphed portrait resembles two people at once, and a rewritten field keeps the original font and background.

The first line does not work unaided. GDRFA Dubai has been reported to use electronic passport readers at its counters, and ultraviolet, infrared, chip, and hologram checks at its examination points~\cite{gulfnews2024catch}. Commercial readers of this kind check a document against the specimen of its design~\cite{regula2023templates}, so a new design is handled by adding its specimen to the reader's library rather than by retraining the reader. However, we found no published error rate for such readers on designs outside their library, which is the number an operator needs before trusting a ``clear''. Officers and machines also disagree. In Document Challenge II, an exercise in which Frontex, the European Border and Coast Guard Agency, tested seven document inspection systems with 39 border officers, officers and machines agreed on only 58\% to 70\% of the documents, and inexperienced officers made more mistakes when they overruled the machine than when they accepted its answer~\cite{gariup2019firstline}.

Learned forgery detectors have not filled this gap. On public benchmarks, they detect alterations almost perfectly on the document designs they were trained on and degrade sharply on designs they were not~\cite{boned2024sidtd,guan2024idnetarxiv,korshunov2025fantasyid,korshunov2025deepid}. Each of them judges a single image, so none can compare the presented document with the specimen that the counter already holds.

In this study, we give the detector that comparison. Our detector receives two images, the data page of the presented document and a reference image of its design, together with a mask of the zones where personal data are expected to differ (see Fig.~\ref{fig:idea}). The data page is the page of a travel document that carries the holder's photograph and personal data, called the biographical data page in ICAO Doc~9303~\cite{icao9303p2}. A design is a document type and version that every document of that type shares, such as one country's identity card of a given series. Built on ChangerEx~\cite{fang2023changer}, a network for detecting changes between two images, the detector outputs a map of the altered regions and a score for the refer-or-clear decision. We measure it on public data under three protocols in which the test designs never appear in training, compare it with the same network without the reference, turn its score into a referral decision, and estimate its speed on a desktop-class CPU.

\section{Research Problem, Significance and Objectives}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Research problem}
The first line must refer or clear a travel document within seconds, for designs from more than 80 countries, using readers whose error rates on unseen designs we could not find in any publication. Learned detectors do not solve this problem either. Four benchmarks measure how they degrade on designs absent from training~\cite{boned2024sidtd,guan2024idnetarxiv,korshunov2025fantasyid,korshunov2025deepid}, yet none of the detectors evaluated there can compare a document with the specimen of its design. To the best of our knowledge, no study has given a learned alteration detector such a specimen and measured the effect on those benchmarks against the same detector without it. This study provides that measurement and reports it in the terms of the first line, namely how many genuine documents are referred when a fixed share of altered ones may be missed, and how long a decision takes.

\subsection{Significance}
For GDRFA Dubai, the study offers three things. First, it measures the error of a detector on designs that it has never seen, for a detector that handles a new design as the counter already does, by adding the design's specimen to its library rather than by collecting new training data and retraining the model. Second, it releases the protocol and code needed to repeat this measurement on the DEC's own template library, without any document leaving the DEC. Third, it expresses the result as a referral decision and its time cost, the terms in which an operator can act on it. For the research field, it offers, to the best of our knowledge, the first measurement of specimen conditioning for alteration detection on designs held out from training on public data, with the specimen as the only difference between the method and its baseline. Reference-based document detectors themselves have been proposed before, for example CheckSim~\cite{ghanmi2021checksim} and the detector of Chen et al.~\cite{chen2022domain}.

\subsection{Objectives and research questions}
The aim of this study is to establish, on public data, whether giving a learned alteration detector a reference of the presented document's design lets it generalize to designs that it has never seen, and to report the result as a first-line referral decision with its time cost. We address five research questions.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*.}]
  \item How much does detection performance degrade when a detector without a reference meets designs absent from training, and how much of that degradation does the reference recover when it is the only difference?
  \item How large is the gap between an unseen design from a familiar source (Protocol A) and an unseen source with a new generator, capture, and script (Protocol B)?
  \item Which reference (a registered design median or a blank specimen) and which fusion (difference or attention) improve detection performance?
  \item When a referral threshold chosen on the training designs is applied unchanged to unseen designs, which miss and referral rates result?
  \item Can the detector decide within a first-line time budget on a desktop-class CPU, and how much time does the reference add?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Contributions}
Our contributions are summarized as follows.
\begin{itemize}
  \item We provide, to the best of our knowledge, the first measurement of specimen conditioning for alteration detection on designs held out from training on public data, under three protocols and against the same network without the specimen.
  \item We adapt ChangerEx~\cite{fang2023changer}, a change-detection network, to identity documents through registration to the design, a design-median reference, and a channel that marks the personal-data zones, and we add a referral head.
  \item We turn the detector's score into a refer-or-clear decision with a threshold chosen on the training designs, and report the miss and referral rates that it yields on unseen designs.
  \item We measure the time per document and the throughput of every variant on a desktop-class CPU.
  \item We release the references, masks, splits, protocol files, and code, so that GDRFA Dubai can repeat the measurement on its own template library.
\end{itemize}

\section{Theoretical and Conceptual Framework and Literature Review}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Conceptual framework}
Our framework rests on three ideas, namely how travel documents are inspected at a border, how a detector's score becomes a decision, and why a detector should compare a document with a reference.

\textbf{Inspection at the border.} Border inspection distinguishes the first-line check, which every traveler passes, from second-line checks, which are further checks carried out in a separate place when the first line has a doubt~\cite{eu2022handbook}. ICAO Doc~9303\footnote{ICAO Doc 9303, Machine Readable Travel Documents, is the international specification for passports and other travel documents (\url{https://www.icao.int/publications/doc-series/doc-9303}).} likewise defines three levels of document inspection, namely a cursory examination for rapid inspection at the point of usage, an examination by trained inspectors with simple equipment, and an inspection by forensic specialists~\cite{icao9303p2}. Dubai's three tiers, with first-line officers, a second line, and the DEC, follow these levels~\cite{thenational2024fake}. To verify a document, inspectors compare it with a specimen, a sample of the same document type and version that the issuing State provides to other States for reference~\cite{icao9303p2}. The first line therefore makes a binary decision, either to clear the document or to refer it to the second line, within seconds and with the document reader as its only instrument.

\textbf{From a score to a decision.} A detector serves this decision through a threshold. Let $s$ be the detector's score that a presented document is altered, $t$ a threshold, and $p$ the prevalence, that is, the share of altered documents among those presented. The first line refers a document when $s > t$. Two error rates then describe the decision. The miss rate $M(t) = P(s \le t \mid \text{altered})$ is the share of altered documents that are cleared, and $P(s > t \mid \text{genuine})$ is the share of genuine documents that are referred. In the vocabulary of ISO/IEC~30107-3~\cite{iso30107}, which the document-forgery benchmarks use~\cite{korshunov2025fantasyid}, these are the attack presentation classification error rate (APCER) and the bona fide presentation classification error rate (BPCER). Since genuine documents make up a share $1-p$ of the traffic and altered ones a share $p$, the law of total probability gives the share of all documents that the first line refers,
\begin{equation}
R(t) = (1-p)\,P(s>t \mid \text{genuine}) + p\,P(s>t \mid \text{altered}).
\label{eq:referral}
\end{equation}
The two error rates are standard, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:referral} only combines them into the quantity that sets the workload of the second line. Raising $t$ lowers $R$ but raises $M$, so an operator fixes the largest acceptable miss rate, the miss budget, and accepts the referral rate that comes with it (see Fig.~\ref{fig:referral}). The prevalence $p$ is small and unknown, so $R(t)$ is driven almost entirely by the genuine documents that are referred. We therefore choose $t$ on the training designs and keep it fixed on designs never seen, and we compute the workload of the second line only for assumed values of $p$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.58\textwidth]{fig_referral.pdf}
\caption{The referral trade-off of Eq.~\eqref{eq:referral} for illustrative detectors whose scores follow two Gaussian distributions of equal width, one for genuine and one for altered documents, the textbook model of signal detection~\cite{green1966sdt}. The three curves differ in the distance $d'$ between the two distributions, and a larger $d'$ means a better detector. The prevalence is set to 1\%, and a detector is read at a fixed miss budget (dotted line). The curves are a sketch, not a result.}
\label{fig:referral}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Comparison against a reference.} Comparing a document with a specimen turns forgery detection into change detection, the task of finding where two registered images of the same object differ. Change detection differs from anomaly detection, which flags samples that look unlike normal data without any paired reference, and from out-of-distribution detection, which flags inputs that come from a different distribution than the training data. The same principle of comparison appears in differential morphing-attack detection, which compares a passport photograph with a trusted live capture of the traveler~\cite{scherhag2020dmad,borghi2021double}, and in the inspection of printed circuit boards, where each board is compared with an image of a defect-free board, the golden sample~\cite{fridman2021changechip}. A detector that sees a single image must learn both what each design looks like and how an alteration departs from it, and only the first of these is specific to a design. A registered specimen supplies the design at inference time, so the network only has to learn how an altered field departs from its design, which is the same on every design. Unlike a circuit board, however, a document's personal-data fields are supposed to differ from the specimen, so we pair the reference with a zone mask that marks where differences are expected.

\subsection{Literature review}
We review four strands of work, namely detectors evaluated on public benchmarks, detectors that compare a document with a reference, systems designed for deployment, and the change-detection methods from which our architecture comes. For each strand, we note what it lacks for the first line.

\subsubsection{Single-image detectors and their measured collapse on unseen designs}
Four benchmarks evaluate alteration detection on designs absent from training, and every detector evaluated on them degrades there. SIDTD~\cite{boned2024sidtd} forges the ten designs of MIDV-2020~\cite{bulatov2022midv2020}. Most of its baseline classifiers exceed 96\% accuracy on templates of the designs they were trained on, whereas its few-shot models reach 82.0\% to 91.0\% on four held-out nationalities, even though they are evaluated on video frames and each test episode supplies five labeled genuine and five labeled forged examples of the held-out nationality. In IDNet~\cite{guan2024idnetarxiv}, a detector trained on the West Virginia driver's license and tested on the Arizona one falls from 98\% to 100\% accuracy to 50.6\% on face morphing and 60.9\% on text replacement. On the printed and photographed cards of FantasyID~\cite{korshunov2025fantasyid}, TruFor~\cite{guillaro2023trufor} reaches AUCs of 99.5\% and 99.9\% on two text attacks but 55.8\% on face swaps, and at a threshold set on validation data, it misses 4.9\% of the attacks while rejecting 62.0\% of the genuine cards. In the DeepID challenge~\cite{korshunov2025deepid}, the winning F1-score fell from 0.991 on fantasy cards to 0.719 on 20,000 private real documents, and a 2026 survey by Das et al.~\cite{das2026survey} found an APCER above 24\% on unseen templates for every public model that it tested, including GPT-5 and Gemini. All of these detectors judge a single image, except CoAARC in SIDTD~\cite{boned2024sidtd}, which pairs the query with a randomly drawn genuine document rather than a specimen of its own design and performs worst, at 62.8\%. None of them can compare a document with the specimen of its design, and none reports its time per document.

\subsubsection{Reference-conditioned detectors for documents}
Commercial document readers check each document against the specimen of its design with checks configured for that design, and we found no published error rate for these checks~\cite{regula2023templates}. Learned detectors that take a reference as a second input are rarer, and each addresses a different question. CheckSim~\cite{ghanmi2021checksim} measures the similarity between an aligned document and a reference image of its document model, on four types of French identity documents from a private dataset. Chen et al.~\cite{chen2022domain} compare a questioned document with genuine references of the same template to detect recapture, that is, a genuine document printed or displayed again and photographed, rather than altered content, and they reach an APCER of 6.92\% at a BPCER of 5\% across document types. Al-Ghadi et al.~\cite{alghadi2022guilloche} compare the guilloche pattern of a document, the fine interlaced lines of its background, with that of a genuine document of the same country, and in their follow-up~\cite{alghadi2023fmidv}, which classifies a single image at test time without the reference, the AUC falls to between 50\% and 56\%. Neitthoffer et al.~\cite{neitthoffer2023knowledge} gave a text-recognition network the template of each document type and found that fusing the template through attention generalized to unseen types, with a character error rate of 0.31, whereas adding it as an extra input channel did not, with a character error rate of 0.81. Most recently, Nieto-Hidalgo et al.~\cite{nietohidalgo2026zeroshot} reduced the equal error rate of presentation-attack detection on unseen countries from 26.56\% to 9.07\% by giving the model four genuine samples of the target card. None of these detectors is evaluated on held-out designs for alterations with public data, none outputs a map of the altered region, and none reports its time per document.

\subsubsection{Deployment-oriented systems}
EdgeDoc~\cite{george2025edgedoc} combines a lightweight convolutional backbone with transformer blocks to detect and localize alterations, FLiD~\cite{flid2026} classifies individual fields with a frozen MobileNetV3-Small backbone and a head of 191K parameters, and Idakto's embedded framework~\cite{idakto2025embedded} matches documents against a reference library of 128 countries, which its authors describe as allowing ``near real-time operation''. These systems are designed to be lightweight, yet we found no measured inference time, throughput, or CPU latency for any of them.

\subsubsection{Where the architecture comes from}
In remote sensing, change detection compares two co-registered satellite images with Siamese networks, which process both images with shared weights and subtract their features~\cite{daudt2018fcsiamdiff}. Changer~\cite{fang2023changer} added interaction between the two feature streams and an alignment step based on a learned flow, and the Open-CD toolbox~\cite{li2025opencd} provides these networks with pretrained weights. In industry, optical inspection compares each printed circuit board with its golden sample~\cite{fridman2021changechip}, and in biometrics, differential morphing-attack detection compares a probe image with a trusted reference~\cite{scherhag2020dmad,borghi2021double}. None of this work concerns identity documents. Moreover, these methods treat every difference from the reference as a defect, whereas the personal-data fields of a document are supposed to differ from its specimen.

To the best of our knowledge, no prior study gives a learned alteration detector the specimen of a document's own design, evaluates it on designs absent from training against the same detector without the specimen, and reports the resulting referral decision and its time cost.

\section{Methodology and Procedures}
\label{sec:method}
This section describes the study population and samples, the data preparation, the proposed architecture, the evaluation protocols and metrics, and the time measurement, which together form the population, samples, and tools that the award's guide asks for. Three public datasets, SIDTD~\cite{boned2024sidtd}, FantasyID~\cite{korshunov2025fantasyid}, and IDNet~\cite{guan2024idnet}, are registered to their designs and paired with a reference of the same design and a zone mask. ChangerEx~\cite{fang2023changer} is extended into a reference-conditioned detector, every variant is trained alongside the same network without a reference, all are evaluated under three protocols, and the speed of the detector is estimated on a desktop-class CPU.

\subsection{Study population and samples}
\label{sec:population}
The population of interest is the identity and travel documents presented at a first-line check. Real travelers' documents can neither be used nor released, so we sample this population through three public datasets that pair genuine documents with digitally altered versions of them (see Table~\ref{tab:data}). They test three ways in which a counter meets a design that it has not seen, namely a new design from a familiar source, a new source with a new generator, capture, and script, and a published pair of designs.

\textbf{SIDTD.} SIDTD~\cite{boned2024sidtd} holds the 1,000 templates of ten identity-card and passport designs from MIDV-2020~\cite{bulatov2022midv2020}, with 100 mock identities per design, and 1,222 forgeries of their text fields. Of these, 145 were made by crop-and-replace, which pastes a field from another document of the same design, and 1,077 by inpaint-and-rewrite, which erases a field and redraws the same value in a different font. The genuine templates are mock documents that were themselves edited digitally when MIDV-2020 was made, so we call them baseline-unmodified mock documents.

\textbf{FantasyID.} FantasyID~\cite{korshunov2025fantasyid} contains 13 fictional designs in ten scripts, Arabic among them, printed on PVC cards and captured with two phones and an office scanner. Its official test set holds 300 genuine captures of three designs that are absent from its training set (Chinese, Dutch, and Singaporean), 149 text rewrites of those designs (Attack-3), and 786 text rewrites of its ten training designs (Attack-1). Its 150 face swaps (Attack-2) are outside the scope of this paper.

\textbf{IDNet.} IDNet~\cite{guan2024idnet,guan2024idnetarxiv} contains synthetic documents of 20 designs. We use its Arizona and West Virginia driver's licenses, each with 5,979 genuine images and as many per attack class, from the public release of 795~$\times$~487 JPEG images. Its ten European designs are the ten designs of MIDV-2020~\cite{bulatov2022midv2020}, so training on them would show the model the four designs that Protocol A holds out for testing. We therefore use only its two US designs.

Every split keeps related images together. A forgery is assigned to the same split as the genuine document that it was made from and, for crop-and-replace, the document that supplied the pasted field. In FantasyID~\cite{korshunov2025fantasyid}, the three captures of one card, by two phones and a scanner, also stay in the same split. This grouping prevents the model from seeing the same document in training and testing.

\subsection{Tools: data preparation}
\label{sec:prep}
Every image becomes a record of its design, identity, label, attack type, capture device, field rectangles, mask of the altered region, and group of related images. We use the forgeries as the datasets provide them and create none of our own.

\textbf{Registration.} Registration places every document in the same position as the reference of its design, so that a pixel-wise comparison is meaningful. Each image is first scaled onto a 512~$\times$~800 canvas, with padding that keeps its proportions. It is then aligned to its design in two passes and without any annotation. Each pass matches SIFT keypoints~\cite{lowe2004sift} between the image and the design and estimates a similarity transform, that is, a rotation, a scale, and a shift, with RANSAC~\cite{fischler1981ransac}, which discards false matches. The masks of the altered regions and the zone masks are moved with the same transforms. An image is aligned only if at least 40 keypoint matches agree with the transform, and every image in our data met this requirement, with at least 214 matches. FantasyID's photographs needed corrections of up to 2.8 degrees and 24 pixels, whereas SIDTD's templates moved by less than one pixel.

\textbf{References.} The default reference of a design is the pixel-wise median over its registered genuine documents (see Fig.~\ref{fig:references}). The median keeps what all documents of the design share, such as the background pattern, headings, field labels, and borders, and it averages out the personal data that differ from one holder to the next. To keep a query's own document out of its reference, the genuine documents of each design are split into two halves by group, and a query is always paired with the median of the other half. Real specimens are not public, and none of the forgery datasets includes one, so the median serves as our public stand-in for a specimen. For the ten SIDTD designs and the two IDNet designs, we also test a second kind of reference, the blank templates that the IDNet authors produced by digitally removing the personal data from one public sample of each design~\cite{guan2024idnetarxiv}, registered to our medians. FantasyID has no such template.

\textbf{Zone and ground-truth masks.} The zone mask of a design marks its personal-data fields, namely the portrait, the text fields, and the signature, as the union of the field rectangles of its genuine documents, enlarged by 4 pixels. The ground-truth mask of a forgery is the rectangle of its altered region, taken from SIDTD's field rectangle enlarged by 8 pixels, from IDNet's bounding boxes, and from FantasyID's rectangles marked as altered.

\textbf{Training pairs.} Each training example pairs a document with the reference of its design. Genuine documents are labeled to be cleared and altered ones to be referred. In addition, 10\% of the genuine training documents are paired with the reference of a different design and labeled to be referred, with the whole document as the altered region. These examples teach the network that a reference that does not match the document is itself a reason to refer, so that a document matched to the wrong design is not cleared by mistake.

\textbf{Compression control.} JPEG quality turns out to reveal the label in both SIDTD and FantasyID. SIDTD stores its genuine templates at quality 92 and every forgery at quality 75, and FantasyID stores its genuine cards at quality 93.5 to 95 and most attacks at quality 75. The quality of a file alone therefore separates genuine from altered images in every split of Protocols A and B (see Table~\ref{tab:jpeg}), so a detector could score perfectly without looking at the document. The DeepID challenge reported this pattern for FantasyID's training data~\cite{korshunov2025deepid}, and we did not find it reported for SIDTD. We therefore re-encode every canvas at quality 95, which removes the measurable trace of the original compression, since a statistic of the 8-pixel JPEG block grid separates the classes with an AUC of 0.971 on the original files but only 0.513 on our canvases. We also re-compress both inputs at random qualities between 60 and 100 during training, and we report every result twice, on our canvases and after re-compressing both classes at quality 75. A large difference between the two would mean that the model had learned the compression rather than the alteration.

\subsection{Tools: proposed architecture}
\label{sec:arch}
ChangerEx~\cite{fang2023changer} is a Siamese change-detection network for satellite images, available with pretrained weights in the Open-CD toolbox~\cite{li2025opencd}. We build on it for two reasons. Change detection has the same structure as our task, two registered images and the question of where they differ, and ChangerEx aligns the two feature maps before comparing them, which absorbs the small misalignment that remains after registration. To our knowledge, ChangerEx has not been applied to documents before, and the building blocks that we keep come from published work~\cite{daudt2018fcsiamdiff,fang2023changer}. We use the network as it is and test an attention variant alongside it (see Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}).

\textbf{Standard network.} The query $x$ and the reference $r$, each with the zone mask $z$ appended as a fourth channel, pass through a shared ResNet-18 encoder~\cite{he2016resnet}. After the second, third, and fourth stages, ChangerEx exchanges alternate columns or channels between the two streams, which lets each stream see the other without extra parameters. The decoder then combines the four scales of each image, predicts a small displacement field for each image, warps each feature map by it, and subtracts the other image's features, a step that its authors call flow dual-alignment fusion. The difference is near zero where the document matches its design and non-zero at an altered field, on any design, and the zone channel tells the network where personal data are allowed to differ. A classifier turns the result into an alteration map $M$ at a quarter of the input resolution. A referral head that we add pools the fused features over the whole image, appends the largest value of $M$, and maps the result through two dense layers to the score $s$.

\textbf{Attention variant.} The attention variant adds, at the two deepest stages, a self-attention block over the query's features and a cross-attention block in which the query's features attend to the reference's features~\cite{vaswani2017attention}, so that each location of the document can look up what its design shows there. A final attention step relates the pooled global feature to the local features before the referral score. Each block has six heads with a key dimension of 32, and its output projection starts at zero, so that the variant starts training as the standard network. This mirrors the attention path of our writer-identification architecture~\cite{abushahla2026ijdar} and follows Neitthoffer et al.~\cite{neitthoffer2023knowledge}, who found attention necessary where channel stacking failed.

\textbf{Control without a reference.} To isolate the effect of the reference, each network is also trained as a control. The control receives a content-free reference canvas, a uniform image at the mean color of the ImageNet normalization, together with the query's zone mask, so it has the same architecture and parameters as the method but never sees the design.

\textbf{Training.} We start from the ChangerEx weights trained on the LEVIR-CD change-detection dataset~\cite{chen2020levir} and widen the first convolution to four input channels with zero weights for the zone channel, so that the network initially behaves as the original one. These weights were trained from random initialization rather than from ImageNet, and their authors selected them on the LEVIR-CD test split, so we use them only as a starting point. The loss has two parts. The first is the binary cross-entropy between the referral score and the label of the document. The second supervises the alteration map pixel by pixel, as the sum of a binary cross-entropy and a Dice loss over the valid region of the canvas. The Dice loss~\cite{milletari2016vnet} is one minus a soft version of the Dice coefficient~\cite{dice1945},
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Dice}} = 1 - \frac{2\sum_i p_i\, g_i + 1}{\sum_i p_i + \sum_i g_i + 1},
\label{eq:dice}
\end{equation}
where $p_i$ is the predicted probability that pixel $i$ is altered, $g_i$ is 1 inside the true altered region and 0 elsewhere, and the sums run over the valid pixels of all the documents in a batch. The coefficient measures the overlap between the predicted and the true altered regions, from 0 when they do not overlap to 1 when they coincide. An altered field covers only a small part of a document, so a pixel-wise cross-entropy alone is dominated by the many unaltered pixels and can be lowered by predicting no alteration anywhere. The Dice loss depends only on the overlap with the altered region, so it rewards finding that region whatever its size, and EdgeDoc~\cite{george2025edgedoc} combines the two losses in the same way to localize forgeries in identity documents. The 1 added to the numerator and the denominator keeps the loss defined for batches that contain no altered pixel. We weight the loss of the map three times as much as the loss of the score, which gives the dense supervision of the map more influence on training than the single label of each document. Table~\ref{tab:train} lists the other settings. Training ran on NVIDIA A10G GPUs of the high-performance computing cluster of the American University of Sharjah. We train every model for the full schedule without early stopping and keep the checkpoint with the best AUC on a validation split of the training designs.

\subsection{Evaluation protocols and metrics}
\label{sec:protocols}
We evaluate under three protocols, as our writer-identification study~\cite{abushahla2026ijdar} did with its line-level and page-disjoint protocols, so that the difference between protocols is itself a result (see Table~\ref{tab:protocols}). In every protocol, the test designs never appear in training, and the reference of a document never contains the document itself.

\textbf{Protocol A, an unseen design from a familiar source.} We train on six SIDTD~\cite{boned2024sidtd} nationalities (Albania, Spain, Estonia, Greece, Serbia, and Slovakia) and test on the other four (Azerbaijan, Finland, Latvia, and Russia), following the official few-shot split of SIDTD. Unlike the published few-shot models, ours see no example of the test designs before testing. SIDTD's published results on this split are therefore not directly comparable to ours, since they were measured on video frames rather than templates and with labeled examples of the test designs.

\textbf{Protocol B, an unseen source, generator, capture, and script.} We train on all ten SIDTD designs and test on FantasyID~\cite{korshunov2025fantasyid} without any training on it, so all 13 of its designs are unseen. The main result is on its three held-out designs, with their 300 genuine cards and 149 text rewrites (Attack-3). Attack-1, the 786 text rewrites of its ten other designs, forms a second result, with the Arabic cards reported separately. \textbf{Metrics.} The area under the ROC curve (AUC) measures how well the scores rank altered documents above genuine ones over all possible thresholds. It compares detectors without fixing an operating point, and most published results report it. However, it says nothing about the threshold that an officer would use, and it does not reveal whether the scores of different designs share a common scale~\cite{fawcett2006roc}. We therefore also report error rates at operating points, namely the equal error rate (EER), at which the miss rate equals the share of genuine documents referred, APCER at a BPCER of 1\% and 5\%, the half total error rate (HTER) at FantasyID's published operating point, and accuracy at a threshold of 0.5 where a published result uses it~\cite{iso30107}. At the pixel level, the F1-score between the predicted map and the box masks measures whether the detector finds the altered field. For the decision itself, the main measure is the transported threshold. We choose the largest threshold whose miss rate on the validation split of the training designs is at most 5\%, keep it fixed, and report the miss and referral rates that it yields on the unseen designs. We report the workload of the second line only for assumed prevalences of 0.1\%, 1\%, and 5\%.

\textbf{Statistics and ablations.} Each result is the mean and standard deviation over three random seeds. For the difference between the method and its control, we also compute a 95\% confidence interval with a paired bootstrap~\cite{efron1993bootstrap}. We repeatedly resample the test set with replacement, keeping each group of related documents together, score both models on the same resample, and take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resulting differences in AUC. Ablations compare the registered median with the blank template and the standard network with the attention variant, and they add, with one seed each, a single genuine card as the reference, an unregistered median, and a network without the zone channel. The runs proceed in priority order, Protocol A first, then Protocol B, then Protocol C.

\subsection{Speed on a desktop-class CPU}
\label{sec:deploy}
We do not know the computers at the counters of GDRFA Dubai, so we estimate the detector's speed on ordinary desktop-class CPUs (see Fig.~\ref{fig:system}). Each variant is timed with a batch size of one, in 32-bit floating point, with four threads. After 20 warm-up runs, we time 200 documents and report the median and the 95th percentile of each stage, namely decoding, resizing, registration, and the network, and of the whole decision, together with the throughput in documents per second, the number of parameters, and the file size. We also time the network with the reference's features computed in advance, since the reference of a design is fixed and its features can be stored when the design is added to the library, which leaves only the query to be processed at the counter. Our target is under one second per document.

Without a reference, the network is close to chance on the four unseen designs, with an AUC of 0.53 (see Table~\ref{tab:resA}). Given the registered design median, the same network reaches an AUC of 0.83, with a spread of only 0.002 across seeds, and the F1-score of its alteration map rises from 0.45 to 0.78. The reference is therefore what makes detection on unseen designs possible (RQ1). The results on canvases re-compressed at quality 75 differ by at most 0.01, so the model did not learn the JPEG shortcut. The blank template also helps, with an AUC of 0.72, but less than the median, probably because the templates come from figures of the IDNet paper and differ from the test images in how they were produced, whereas the median is built from images of the same kind as the test images. The attention variant does not improve on the standard network. With the median, it is 0.03 lower in AUC, three times the spread across seeds, and lower in map F1, so the standard network is our method and the attention variant remains an ablation (RQ3).

By attack type, the median reference reaches an AUC of 0.88 on inpaint-and-rewrite forgeries but only 0.51 on the 68 crop-and-replace forgeries of the test set, for every variant. A crop-and-replace forgery pastes a field from another document of the same design, so the pasted field matches the design in font, color, and layout, and a comparison with the design cannot reveal it. By design, the AUC with the median reference is 0.93 for Latvia, 0.88 for Azerbaijan, 0.86 for Finland, and 0.65 for the Russian internal passport, whose genuine documents vary the most in print.

\subsection{Referral analysis}
In Protocol A, the threshold chosen on the validation split of the training designs at a 5\% miss rate does not transfer. On the unseen designs, it misses no altered document but refers 99\% to 100\% of the genuine ones, since the scores of an unseen design are shifted as a whole relative to the training designs, which makes a threshold set on the training designs too strict for them. A threshold chosen on the unseen designs themselves would refer 85\% of the genuine documents at a 5\% miss rate and 55\% at a 10\% miss rate. The scores therefore rank documents well within a design, but their level depends on the design, so the operating point has to be set for each design (RQ4). Calibrating it from a few genuine documents of each new design, as Nieto-Hidalgo et al.~\cite{nietohidalgo2026zeroshot} did with four genuine samples, is the natural next step. \todo{Protocols B and C, the referral curves, and the workload for assumed prevalences of 0.1\%, 1\%, and 5\%.}

\subsection{Discussion and limitations}
\todo{Discussion of the results across protocols once Protocols B and C are in.}

\textbf{Limitations.} Several limitations bound what these measurements show. All training forgeries come from two generators, and capture is tested only by FantasyID, so the results describe robustness to a change of design, source, and capture rather than to a skilled forger. SIDTD's genuine images are edited mock documents, IDNet is used in its reduced public release, our blank templates were produced digitally by the IDNet authors rather than issued by States as specimens, and the masks are boxes. A comparison with the design cannot reveal a field copied from another document of the same design, as the crop-and-replace forgeries show. Face swaps and morphs are outside the scope of this paper, and their natural reference is the traveler's live face rather than the specimen~\cite{scherhag2020dmad}. Finally, the DEC's ratio of irregularities is not a prevalence, so workload can only be projected for assumed prevalences, and a detector that sees an RGB image cannot verify ultraviolet, infrared, or chip features, which remain the task of the reader.
